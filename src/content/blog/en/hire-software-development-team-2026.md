Hiring a software team ties your business to that team for months or years. This article summarizes what to know before you start, from a team that builds systems for both SMEs and enterprises.

## The phases of a real software project

Most projects that succeed go through five stages.

1. **Discovery.** Agree on what problem the system solves, who uses it, and how success is measured. This stage is short but the most important: a misunderstanding here gets more expensive every week it survives.
2. **Design.** Both the screens (UX/UI) and the system architecture. A good team presents options with trade-offs instead of one take-it-or-leave-it proposal.
3. **Iterative build.** Working software should be delivered in usable slices every one to two weeks, so you see the real thing early and can steer. A team that disappears for six months and returns with a finished system is a gamble.
4. **Launch.** Real-user testing, data migration, and a rollback plan if something goes wrong.
5. **Operate.** Production software needs ongoing care: bug fixes, security updates, and changes as the business evolves.

## Common pricing models

- **Fixed price.** Works when the scope is genuinely fixed. The downside: every change becomes a renegotiation, and the vendor is incentivized to cut corners to protect margin.
- **Time & materials.** You pay for actual hours or days. More flexible and transparent, provided the team reports progress regularly. Suits projects where requirements are still evolving.
- **Retainer.** A monthly commitment of team capacity. Suits products that need continuous development and long-term operation.

Most teams price by team size and duration. In Thailand, a small business system typically starts in the low hundreds of thousands of baht; complex or AI-heavy systems usually run into the millions. The real number depends on scope, so always ask for an itemized quote.

## Questions to ask before signing

- Can I see a real system you delivered that is still in production (not just screenshots)?
- Who owns the source code and infrastructure after delivery?
- Who will actually do the work: how many senior and junior engineers?
- How long is a delivery cycle, and when do I see the first working version?
- How is post-launch support priced, and what is the response time when the system goes down?

## Red flags

- Saying yes to everything without asking questions back. A good team challenges your requirements before accepting them.
- You can only talk to sales, never to the engineers who will build it.
- No demo or staging environment for you to try during development.
- A contract that keeps the source code with the vendor, locking you in permanently.

## Summary

Good software procurement is not about finding the cheapest team. It is about finding one that ships real software in short cycles, is transparent about pricing, and leaves you owning everything. If you are evaluating teams now, use the questions above as a starting checklist.
